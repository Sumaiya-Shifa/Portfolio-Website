 
 
A Lightweight and Explainable Machine Learning Framework 
for SMS Spam Detection with Low False-Positive Rates 
Abstract-SMS filtering must identify abusive messages without hiding legitimate authentication, banking, delivery, or service notifications. 
This study builds a transparent classical benchmark on the 5,572-message spam.csv derivative of the SMS Spam Collection. The recorded 
protocol uses an unstratified 80/20 split (random state 42), lowercase and letter-only normalization, English stop-word removal, WordNet 
lemmatization, and a training-fitted TF-IDF vocabulary capped at 3,000 features. Multinomial Naive Bayes, log istic regression, decision 
tree, k-nearest neighbors, linear support vector machine (SVM), and random forest are compared using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 -
score, false-positive rate, and explicit error counts, mostly at their library-default settings rather than a tuned configuration. On 1,115 test 
messages, Multinomial Naive Bayes records no false positives but 81.33% spam recall. Linear SVM gives the strongest measured balance: 
98.12% accuracy, 97.78% precision, 88.00% recall, 92.63% F1 -score, and three false positives among 965 legitimate messages (0.31%). 
LIME and linear coefficients provide complementary local and global lexical evidence. A data audit also finds 116 exact texts  shared 
across the recorded training and test partitions, so the results  are presented as a benchmark operating point rather than an unbiased 
deployment estimate. The work contributes a readable, false-positive-centered comparison and an explicit account of dataset, explanation, 
and validation limits; it does not claim dedicated smishing detection or measured on-device efficiency. 
Index Terms-SMS spam detection, TF-IDF, false-positive rate, linear SVM, LIME, explainable artificial intelligence, corpus leakage. 
1 INTRODUCTION 
Short Message Service (SMS) still carries one -time passwords, 
account alerts, delivery notices, public -service information, and 
ordinary person -to-person text. That same direct reach is exactly 
what makes it a cheap channel for bulk advertising, scams, and  
social engineering. Content -based SMS filtering became its own 
research problem once labeled short -message corpora and early 
benchmark classifiers appeared [1] to [3]. Later comparative work 
showed that the apparent best model is really a function of corpus 
composition, representation, evaluation design, and the relative 
cost of an error [4], [5] not the classifier alone. 
Accuracy by itself is not the objective. A false negative leaves 
an unwanted or harmful message visible; a false positive can 
suppress a legitimate banking alert or authentication code, and that 
failure is more expensive in almost any real deployment. In a n 
imbalanced corpus dominated by legitimate traffic, a model can 
post a high accuracy figure while still missing a large share of 
spam. A conference -ready evaluation should therefore report 
precision, recall, F1 -score, false -positive rate (FPR), and the 
underlying false -positive (FP) and false -negative (FN) counts 
rather than one aggregate percentage. 
Spam and smishing are not the same thing. Spam is simply an 
unsolicited-message label; smishing normally denotes a phishing 
attempt delivered by SMS to steal information, install malware, or 
induce a harmful action. Security -oriented systems may draw on 
mobile context, URL behavior, or threat -specific labels [6], [7]. 
The benchmark used here contains only ham and spam, so it can 
support binary spam filtering but cannot establish a smishing 
detection rate or separate advertising from credential theft. That 
boundary is kept explicit throughout the study. 
The literature now ranges from hybrid and rapidly selected 
classical models [8], [9], through multi-type feature fusion [10], to 
transformer and BERT -based systems [11] to [ 14]. Recent work 
adds explanation, privacy-preserving learning, adversarial testing, 
large language models, fuzzy reasoning, and explicit 
misclassification analysis [15] to [20]. These advances matter, but 
they do not remove the need for transparent, lower -complexity 
baselines: reported scores are not directly rankable when datasets, 
augmentation, split procedures, labels, or thresholds differ, and 
attaching an explanation doe s not by itself prove that the 
explanation is stable, faithful, or useful. 
This paper addresses a narrower empirical gap: a controlled 
comparison of six common classifiers under the same bounded 
sparse representation, with false-positive behavior treated as a first-
class selection criterion and with local and global lexical evide nce 
reported at the same operating point. LIME [21] supplies a local 
surrogate explanation, while direct linear coefficients provide a 
global view comparable in spirit but not identical in assumptions to 
additive attribution approaches such as SHAP [22]. The design 
favors auditability and reproducibility of the representation over 
novelty of the classifiers, and it takes for granted that spam 
vocabulary drifts over time and that a static random split ca nnot 
test that drift [23]. 
Three research questions guide the analysis. RQ1: what 
precision–recall and FP, FN  trade-offs arise across the six 
classifiers under one 3,000 -feature TF-IDF representation? RQ2: 
which model gives the strongest measured balance between spam 
recall and false-alarm suppression on the recorded test split? RQ3: 
do the local and global expla nations produce plausible lexical 
evidence, and which conclusions can they not support? 
The contribution is fourfold. First, the exact 5,572 -row corpus 
derivative and fixed experimental partition are preserved and 
documented. Second, a controlled six -model comparison is 
reported with both rates and counts. Third, local LIME evidence 
and global SVM weights are joined in a single audit trail. Fourth, 
exact duplicate overlap and other threats to validity are identified 
and reported rather than smoothed over. 
2 LITERATURE REVIEW 
Early SMS filtering carried ideas from email spam research over to 
much shorter texts. Gómez Hidalgo et al. [2] showed that message 
content alone could support supervised filtering even in a sparse, 
heavily abbreviated vocabulary. Cormack et al. [3] examined spam 
filtering for short messages further, helping establish the field's 
comparative-experimentation norms. Almeida et al. [1] 
consolidated several sources into the SMS Spam Collection and 
compared established learners on it, producing the benchmark that 
underlies most later work. Delany et al. [4] reviewed methods and 
data in more depth, reinforcing that a result is inseparable from its 
corpus and evaluation protocol. 
Classical text classifiers stay plausible for SMS because 
recurring campaign vocabulary creates strong lexical regularities: 
probabilistic models exploit token likelihoods, regularized linear 
models combine many weak indicators, margin classifiers tend to 
do well in high -dimensional sparse spaces, and tree - or instance-
based methods add contrasting nonlinear and local biases. Their 
training and inference paths are also easier to inspect than a deep 
contextual encoder's. Blanzieri and Bryl's survey [5] is a useful 
check on that optimism, though: spam is nonstationary, class costs 
differ, and benchmark choice can dominate the apparent 
performance gap. All three cautions carry over directly to SMS. 
 
 
The benchmark's popularity creates a second problem: corpus 
derivatives are not always identical. Files can differ by encoding, 
auxiliary columns, duplicate handling, or the two additional ham 
messages now present in the UCI archive. Random record -level 
splitting can also place repeated campaign texts on both sides of 
the boundary, so a classifier meets exact or near -exact language 
during both training and testing  making the hold -out less 
independent than its size suggests. Reusing the collection for 
comparability is legitimate, but a careful report should name the 
file variant, disclose the split procedure, and stop short of treating 
one random partition as a deployment estimate. 
Feature engineering extends the basic bag -of-words pipeline. 
Hybrid systems combine classifiers or join lexical evidence with 
message attributes, as in Baaqeel and Zagrouba [8]; Rapacz et al. 
[9] instead study rapid classifier selection, a reminder that practical 
filtering has computational as well as predictive constraints. Al -
Kabbi et al. [10] integrate multiple feature types through early 
fusion to capture coverage a single word representation might miss. 
Character patterns, message length, punctuation, digits, currency 
symbols, sender information, and URL structure can all carry 
signal in principle. 
Richer features come with trade-offs. Hand-designed attributes 
can capture obfuscation and contact patterns, but every extraction 
stage adds assumptions, maintenance work, and often a 
dependence on metadata that is unavailable or privacy -sensitive. 
Early f usion can widen coverage while making it harder to tell 
which feature family produced a gain; aggressive cleaning keeps a 
pipeline compact but can discard exactly the digits, punctuation, or 
domain syntax a fraudulent message relies on. This study keeps the 
recorded letter-only TF-IDF pipeline so its results stay unchanged, 
and treats the removed signals as a limitation rather than claiming 
the preprocessing is universally optimal. 
Efficiency claims need precise language too. A sparse 3,000 -
feature linear model is architecturally smaller than a transformer, 
and its prediction path is simple  but that alone does not prove low 
latency, memory, or energy use on a phone. Such claims need 
serialized-model size, peak memory, timing under controlled 
hardware, and an energy measurement protocol. "Lightweight" in 
this paper describes a bounded represen tation and a classical 
inference structure; it is not a completed mobile benchmark. 
Smishing research widens the threat model past unsolicited 
content. S-Detector frames the problem as mobile attack detection 
[6]. Mishra and Soni [7] combine SMS content analysis with URL 
behavior, since phishing often depends on where a link leads rather 
than on the words around it. This matters because a harmless 
advertisement and a credential-stealing message can share the same 
urgent or promotional vocabulary while posing very different risks. 
That richer threat model rules out a simple comparison with the 
SMS Spam Collection. A binary spam label does not confirm 
malicious intent, and plenty of legitimate messages contain links or 
urgent language. A security claim would need URL reputation, 
redirects, domain age, landing -page content, sender identity, and 
user interaction. So the present experiment can offer a first -stage 
content filter or a benchmark baseline, but it should not be called a 
validated smishing defense. A future dataset would need explicit 
categories benign bulk, advertising, scam, credential phishing, 
malware delivery  and annotation rules that separate content 
category from security impact. 
Transformer-based work goes after information unigram TF -IDF 
cannot encode. Liu et al. [11] propose a Spam Transformer, Oswald 
et al. [12] use BERT embeddings for intention-driven filtering, and 
Ghourabi and Alohaly [13] combine transformer -based 
embeddings with ensemble learning all following the wider success 
of bidirectional transformer pretraining represented by BERT [14]. 
Contextual encoders can model word order, polysemy, and longer 
dependencies, which can help when a campaign avoids familiar 
keywords. 
Higher representational capacity isn't a free upgrade, though. 
Fine-tuning costs more compute and adds sensitivity to 
initialization, optimization, and checkpoint choice; prompted or 
embedding-based approaches can add service latency, privacy 
exposure, or version dependence. Deep models can also inherit 
corpus leakage and split bias a more sophisticated learner does not 
fix a nonindependent test set. So a claim that one architecture beats 
another only holds up when the same examples, split constraints, 
preprocessing boundaries, and decision criteria are applied to both. 
ExplainableDetector pairs fine -tuned transformer language 
models with explanation analysis and reports very high 
performance under its augmentation -based protocol [15]. That's a 
real demonstration that contextual performance and XAI can 
coexist, but it doe sn't make a bounded classical baseline obsolete: 
augmentation, architecture, and validation choices change the 
experiment, and choosing between systems also needs error counts, 
resource measurements, calibration, and robustness evidence. The 
useful compari son is multidimensional, not a single ranking by 
accuracy. 
Post-hoc explainability asks why a classifier produced a given 
label. LIME perturbs one instance and fits a simpler local surrogate 
around it [21]; SHAP assigns additive feature contributions 
through a game-theoretic framework [22]. Both can reveal whether 
a detector leans on plausible content, but they answer different 
formal questions and can shift with background data, perturbation 
choices, tokenization, or random sampling. A visually convincing 
explanation is not automatically faithful, stable, or causa l local 
surrogate fidelity and repeated-seed stability are things that should 
be measured when explanations are used operationally. 
Recent work treats explanation as more than a presentation 
layer. SpamDam assembles more than 76,000 recent spam 
messages from social platforms and studies centralized and 
federated learning alongside adversarial resistance [16], which 
exposes three weaknesses common to smaller benchmark studies: 
dated vocabulary, privacy constraints on data collection, and 
vulnerability to deliberate manipulation. SpaLLM-Guard evaluates 
open and commercial LLMs under zero -shot, few -shot, fine -
tuning, adversarial, and concept-drift settings, and reports that fine-
tuning beats convenient zero -shot use [17]  relevant to adaptive 
filtering, though its computing and governance needs differ 
substantially from a sparse local model. 
Fuzzy and hybrid studies look for middle ground between 
learned representations and human-interpretable uncertainty. Jasim 
et al. combine fuzzy logic with a bidirectional LSTM [18], and 
Birthriya et al. combine fuzzy reasoning with TF -IDF-oriented 
feature engineering for smishing detection [19]. These designs can 
make linguistic uncertainty explicit, but each added component still 
needs its own ablation to isolate what it actually contributes. X -
MAP takes a different angle: it uses feature attributions and topic 
profiles to trace semantic patterns behind spam and phishing 
misclassifications and to support an error-repair layer [20], shifting 
explainability from describing a prediction toward diagnosing 
systematic failure. 
Temporal robustness is a related concern. Delany et al. [23] 
study concept drift in spam filtering, where vocabulary and 
campaign strategy change over time. Randomly mixing an older 
corpus into training and testing cannot simulate that process. A 
 
 
practical evaluation should split by time or campaign, measure how 
performance decays, and specify an update policy; explanations 
can help monitor drift by showing which tokens gain or lose 
influence, but they don't substitute for temporal validation. 
A comparison table only means something when the underlying 
experimental units are comparable. Studies may use the original 
collection, a CSV derivative, a deduplicated version, an augmented 
training set, a translated corpus, or a newly collected threat dataset 
and may report a random hold -out, a cross -validation average, a 
temporal test, or an adversarial test. Even when the metric shares a 
name, these designs estimate different quantities: accuracy on a 
random record split cannot be ranked fairly against accura cy on a 
future-campaign split, and a spam/ham result is not a smishing 
result. Foundational comparative work already made this point 
about methods and data needing to be read together [1], [4]. 
Error reporting is a second comparability requirement. On a 
ham-dominated test set, two models can post similar accuracy 
while imposing very different costs. Precision describes how 
reliable a positive prediction is, recall describes how much spam 
gets recovered, and FPR describes the share of legitimate traffic 
wrongly blocked none of which communicates sample size on its 
own. Reporting FP and FN counts alongside the denominators 
keeps the operating point auditable and stops a rounded percentage 
from hiding a small or unstable number of events. A zero count 
should be read as zero observed errors, not as proof the population 
rate is zero. 
Independence has to be checked separately from feature -fitting 
leakage. Fitting a vectorizer only on training text is necessary but 
not sufficient  repeated campaign messages can still cross the 
boundary. Grouping exact and near -duplicate templates before 
splitting tests generalization to unseen campaigns more directly, 
and temporal separation asks a further question: does the model 
survive changing vocabulary and attacker behavior, the concern at 
the center of concept -drift research [23]? These controls tend to 
lower the reported score, but they raise the credibility of the 
estimate. 
Reproducibility, finally, is more than a fixed random seed. A 
complete record should identify the exact data file or checksum, 
publish split indices, preserve preprocessing resources, name 
library versions, state every nondefault parameter, and keep the raw 
predictions the metrics were computed from. Probability 
calibration and stochastic ensembles can shift across software 
releases even when the visible code looks unchanged, so a result 
can be computationally repeatable in one archived environment yet 
lack external validity  or be externally relevant yet hard to 
reproduce. Both dimensions deserve their own report rather than 
being folded into the single word "reproducible." 
Taken as a whole, the literature shows SMS filtering being 
approached through classical text models, engineered features, 
contextual encoders, threat -specific signals, hybrid uncertainty 
mechanisms, and post-hoc explanations. Four gaps stand out. First, 
many reported scores are hard to interpret without the exact corpus 
derivative and split. Second, accuracy still gets more attention than 
the operational FP–FN balance. Third, explanation studies tend to 
focus on either individual predictions or model-level patterns rather 
than presenting both with clear scope limits. Fourth, architectural 
efficiency sometimes substitutes for a measured deployment cost. 
This paper does not claim to close every one of those gaps or 
introduce a new classifier. Its contribution is a transparent baseline 
that holds representation and test set constant across six common 
learners, selects an operating point from explicit false -alarm and 
miss counts, and pairs local and global lexical evidence while also 
auditing the file and partition for duplicate overlap. Table 1 
positions this contribution against representative studies and 
explains why their reported performance figures are not simply 
copied into one ranking table.

3 METHODOLOGY 
3.1 End-to-end project workflow 
The project turns a raw SMS message into a ham or spam 
prediction and then produces human -readable evidence for the 
selected classifier. Figure 1 lays out the full experiment in eight 
stages: dataset loading, data audit and partitioning, text 
preprocessing, TF-IDF feature construction, model training, fixed-
test prediction, performance evaluation, and explanation. The first 
five stages build the trained candidate models; the remaining three 
compare their frozen predictions and examine the model chosen 
from the recorded results. Test labels are used only to compute 
evaluation measures  they never update the vocabulary, the 
classifier parameters, or the explanations. 
During data preparation, the CSV is reduced to the class label 
and message text, labels are mapped to ham = 0 and spam = 1, and 
exact duplicate counts are recorded before the preserved 80/20 
random split is applied. The same cleaning function then strips non-
alphabetic characters, lowercases each message, removes English 
stop words, and lemmatizes what's left. A TF -IDF vectorizer is 
fitted only on the cleaned training messages; its stored vocabulary 
and inverse -document-frequency values then transform both the 
training and held -out messages into vectors of 3,000 or fewer 
components. 
Six classifiers receive the same training matrix and labels, and 
each fitted model predicts the same 1,115 held-out messages, so the 
representation and the evaluation population stay constant across 
all six. Accuracy, precision, recall, F1 -score, FPR, FNR, and the 
FP/FN counts are then computed from those predictions, with 
particular attention to false positives, since a legitimate 
authentication or banking message can be costly to hide. Linear 
SVM is carried into the explanation stage because it posts the 
highest recorded accuracy, recall, and F1 -score while producing 
only three false positives. 
Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is applied after 
prediction, purely to make the model's evidence legible to a 
reviewer it does not retrain the classifier or change a predicted 
label. The local view asks why the model treated one particular 
SMS as spam or ham; the global view asks which vocabulary terms 
generally push the fitted linear boundary toward spam. LIME 
supplies th e local view and the learned linear SVM coefficients 
supply the global one; Section 3.7 explains how each is 
constructed, how to read the sign of each value, and why neither 
should be read as proof of malicious intent. 
In a future deployment, a new SMS would enter at the 
preprocessing stage, pass through the stored TF-IDF transform, and 
receive a label from the selected classifier; a local explanation 
could be generated on review, and the global coefficient list could 
support periodic model audits. The present work evaluates the 
classification-and-explanation pipeline offline only  it does not 
claim that a smartphone interface, a URL -analysis layer, or an on-
device performance benchmark has already been built.

3.2 Dataset variant and label scope 
Experiments use the widely circulated spam.csv derivative of the 
SMS Spam Collection [1], distributed in a related form by the UCI 
Machine Learning Repository [24]. The file contains 5,572 English 
messages: 4,825 ham (86.59%) and 747 spam (13.41%). The 
current UCI text archive contains two additional ham records, 
which is why this particular derivative is named rather than just the 
collection. Auxiliary empty columns are discarded, labels are 
mapped to y = 0 for ham and y = 1 for spam, and no attempt is 
made to infer a separate smishing label. 
A file -level audit finds 5,169 unique message texts and 403 
duplicate label –text rows, with no identical text carrying 
conflicting labels. These figures describe exact duplicates only they 
do not count near -duplicates with changed numbers, spelling, or 
punctuation. The audit matters because repeated campaign 
templates can make a random record -level evaluation look better 
than it is, even when feature fitting is correctly restricted to t he 
training set. 
3.3 Fixed split and leakage boundaries 
The recorded experiment uses train_test_split with test_size = 0.20 
and random_state = 42, without stratification, producing 4,457 
training messages (3,860 ham, 597 spam) and 1,115 test messages 
(965 ham, 150 spam). Table 2 reports this composition, and Ta ble 
3 spells out every use of the fixed test set. The TF-IDF vocabulary 

 
 
is learned only from training messages, and the fitted transform is 
applied unchanged to the test set, which rules out direct vocabulary 
fitting on held-out text. 
The split is nevertheless not independent at the message level: 
116 exact text strings occur in both partitions. This partition is 
preserved anyway, because the recorded result table and confusion 
counts were generated from it  quietly changing the split would 
invalidate those reported values. Instead, the overlap is disclosed 
and treated as a threat to external validity. A stronger confirmatory 
experiment would group exact and near -duplicate campaigns 
before splitting and keep a  separate validation set for threshold 
selection.

3.4 Text preprocessing 
Each raw message first has every character outside the English 
alphabet replaced by a space, then is lowercased and split into 
individual tokens. Common English stop words are removed and 
the remaining tokens are lemmatized with WordNet [25] in the 
same pass. Empty auxiliary columns are never used. This sequence 
matches the recorded notebook and is applied identically to both 
partitions before feature extraction. 
The procedure trims case and inflectional variation, but it is not 
a neutral choice. Stripping every non -letter character deletes 
telephone numbers, currency amounts, punctuation intensity, and 
URL syntax  all signals that could matter for fraud or campaign 
detection and English stop words can still carry pragmatic weight 

 
 
in a message this short. These choices are kept for result 
consistency and documented in Table 4, so a later ablation can 
determine which transformations actually help versus quietly hurt. 
3.5 Bounded TF-IDF representation 
The normalized messages are encoded with scikit -learn's TF-IDF 
vectorizer [26], called with its library defaults except for a 
vocabulary capped at the 3,000 highest -ranked training features; 
inverse document frequency is smoothed and each document 
vector is L2-normalized by default. One consequence worth stating 
plainly: the vectorizer's default tokenizer only keeps tokens of two 
or more word characters, so common single -letter texting 
shorthand (“u,” “i”) never becomes a feature. Only training text

Explainable artificial intelligence, in this context, means 
methods that expose the input evidence behind a prediction in a 
form a person can actually inspect  a different question from 
performance evaluation. Accuracy and recall say how often 
predictions agree with labels; an explanation says which 
represented words influenced one decision or the fitted boundary 
as a whole. That evidence can reveal reliance on sensible campaign 
terms or on suspicious shortcuts, but it cannot prove the message is 
malicious, that the prediction is correct, or that the same evidence 
will still matter on future data. 
The local explanation comes from Local Interpretable Model -
agnostic Explanations (LIME) [21]. One test message x0 and the 
selected SVM prediction are fixed; LIME generates nearby 
versions of that message by keeping or dropping tokens; the trained 
classifier scores each perturbed version for spam; perturbations 
closer to the original get larger locality weights; and a small 
weighted linear surrogate g is fit to imitate the classifier only in that 
neighborhood. With candidate interpretable models G, locality 
weights π, a fidelity loss L, and a complexity penalty Ω, the 
objective is

A positive LIME weight means the presence of that token 
pushes the local surrogate toward spam; a negative weight pushes 
it toward ham, and its magnitude reflects strength within that one 
neighborhood only  it says nothing about the dataset as a whole, 
since LIME approximates the classifier near a single message 
rather than opening up the full SVM. Local fidelity is how well g 
reproduces f on the weighted perturbations; stability is how much 
the explanation c hanges when the perturbation sample or random 
seed changes. The archived experiment keeps one recorded 
explanation but no repeated-seed fidelity or stability measurement.

5 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 
5.1 Limitations 
The selected operating point is conditional on both the recorded test 
split and on whatever relative cost gets assigned to each error type. 
KNN and MNB share 100% observed precision, but KNN misses 
85 of 150 spam messages. The decision tree recovers eight more 
spam messages than MNB while adding 15 false positives. 
Random forest trims one false positive off SVM's count but misses 
five more spam messages. Linear SVM is preferred here for its 
measured balance, but a different application might reasonably 
prefer MNB if blocking any legitimate message carries a much 
steeper cost. 
The message -length profile is a property of this particular 
historical corpus, not a universal rule. Spam messages in the fixed 
test set run a median 147 raw characters versus 52 for ham, with 
means near 137.5 and 72.8 respectively. Length isn't used as a 
model feature here, and a long message can perfectly well be a 
legitimate notification, but the gap does suggest campaign -level 
regularities that may help on this dataset and weaken as messaging 
styles change. 
Comparing this work against published results is limited by 
incompatible experimental settings. The strong showing from 
MNB, SVM, and random forest lines up with foundational studies 
of content features and conventional learners [1] –[4], [8], [9], but 
that is not evidence that sparse models beat contextual systems 
[11]–[15] those studies differ in corpus construction, augmentation, 
feature representation, hyperparameter selection, and validation. 
Smishing studies [6], [7], [19] also target a richer security label 
than the plain spam/ham used here, so their numbers can't be placed 
in a fair ranking without a shared dataset and protocol. 
Internal validity is limited by a single unstratified random split, 
unpinned software versions, no separate validation set, and mostly-
default hyperparameters rather than nested model selection. The 
corpus carries 403 duplicate label–text rows, and 116 exact texts sit 
in both the recorded training and test partitions an overlap that can 
reward recognition of repeated campaign language rather than 
genuine generalization. Removing or grouping those duplicates 

 
 
would change every reported count, so this paper reports the 
overlap instead of quietly running a different experiment. 
External validity is limited by one older English corpus, dated 
regional vocabulary, and coarse binary labels; the design says 
nothing about future campaigns, adversarial spelling, multilingual 
messages, sender behavior, URL destinations, or out -of-domain 
traffic. Construct validity is limited to accuracy, and F1-score don't 
capture the real cost of hiding an authentication code or missing a 
credential-stealing link. FPR and the raw error counts clarify the 
trade-off that was recorded here, but a genuine user -impact study 
and an application-specific cost model are still missing. 
The preprocessing stage removes digits, punctuation, emoji, and 
URL structure, all of which may carry fraud signal it discards. The 
local LIME evidence comes from a single correctly classified 
message with no recorded fidelity or repeated -seed stability score, 
and the global coefficient view identifies learned associations 
without establishing causation or fairness. Finally, the bounded TF-
IDF representation suggests a compact inference path, but 
serialized size, memory use, latency, energy consumption, 
calibration, and usability were never measured on an actual mobile 
device. 
5.2 Future work 
The natural next experiment groups exact and near -duplicate 
templates before creating separate training, validation, and test 
partitions. Repeated stratified group splits or campaign-level cross-
validation could estimate variance across samples, and a 
chronological hold -out could measure performance on later 
vocabulary. The data checksum, split indices, preprocessing 
resources, package versions, and random seeds should all be 
archived together, so this design would show how much of the 
recorded performance survives once repeated campaign content is 
isolated. 
Feature ablation should then restore digits, punctuation, 
character n -grams, message length, URL tokens, and sender or 
domain signals one family at a time. A threat -labeled dataset —
separating benign personal messages, legitimate bulk notifications, 
advertising, scams, credential phishing, and malware delivery  is a 
further necessary step, as is coverage of multilingual and code -
mixed messages, relevant for settings such as Bangladesh where 
Bangla, English, and transliterated text often share an inbox. Under 
a shared protocol, this linear SVM baseline could then be compared 
fairly against contextual, ensemble, fuzzy, and large -language-
model approaches. 
The explanation pipeline needs direct evaluation of its own. 
Future work should report LIME surrogate fidelity, repeat each 
explanation across random seeds, and measure agreement among 
LIME, exact linear contributions, and SHAP -style attributions 
[21], [22]. Grouping misclassified messages by campaign and error 
type would help show whether the model leans on obsolete terms, 
misses new obfuscation, or mistakes legitimate promotions for 
spam—and a small user study could test whether showing 
supporting and opposing tokens together helps a reviewer without 
the explanation being mistaken for a security verdict. 
Deployment-oriented work should serialize the vectorizer and 
classifier, profile memory and latency on representative low-power 
phones, and measure energy use under realistic message volumes. 
A recoverable quarantine folder is a safer default than automati c 
deletion, since it lets a user restore a false positive; suspicious 
URLs could be routed to a separate reputation or landing -page 
analyzer, and decision thresholds should be chosen on validation 
data according to the relative cost of a false alarm versus  a missed 
threat. Monitoring global coefficients and recent error samples over 
time could also flag vocabulary drift and signal when retraining is 
due. 
6 CONCLUSION 
This study built a complete, inspectable machine -learning 
workflow for binary SMS spam detection. The methodology starts 
from the 5,572 -message spam.csv derivative of the SMS Spam 
Collection, records its duplicate and label properties, preserves the 
documented 80/20 split, and applies one preprocessing function 
identically to both partitions. A TF -IDF vectorizer fitted only on 
the 4,457 training messages produces a sparse representation 
capped at 3,000 features; Multinomial Naive Bayes, logistic 
regression, decision tree, KNN, linear SVM, and random forest are 
then trained on that common representation and evaluated on the 
same 1,115 held -out messages, which makes their FP and FN 
behavior directly comparable. 
The results make the case for why accuracy alone falls short on 
an imbalanced task like this one. KNN produces no observed false 
positives but catches only 43.33% of spam. MNB also produces no 
false positives while lifting recall to 81.33%, making it a defensible 
conservative alternative. Linear SVM gives the strongest overall 
balance recorded here: it correctly identifies 132 of 150 spam 
messages, misses 18, and incorrectly flags three of 965 ham 
messages 98.12% accuracy, 97.78% precision, 88.00% recall, 
92.63% F1 -score, and a 0.31% FPR. That selection reflects both 
spam recovery and legitimate -message protection, not a single 
headline score. 
Explainable artificial intelligence adds an audit layer on top of 
that operating point. LIME approximates the selected SVM near 
one message and shows which present tokens locally push its score 
toward spam or ham  the recorded example assigns the strongest 
positive local influence to ‘pobox ,’ with ‘know’ pulling the other 
way. The global view ranks the fitted linear coefficients and 
surfaces terms such as ‘txt,’ ‘claim,’ ‘urgent,’ and ‘prize’ as general 
spam-associated directions in the learned vocabulary. Together, 
these views let a reviewer inspect both one decision and the broader 
model pattern, but they remain evidence about classifier behavior, 
not proof of sender intent, explanation stability, or causal 
importance. 
The paper's contribution is an auditable baseline with explicit 
operating counts, equations, workflow controls, and clearly stated 
explanation boundaries. Its scope stays limited by the spam/ham 
labels, one historical English corpus, the 116 exact texts sh ared 
across the fixed train-test boundary, letter-only preprocessing, and 
the absence of temporal, adversarial, mobile-performance, and user 
studies. The reported values should be read as results for this 
preserved benchmark, not as guaranteed production r ates or 
validated smishing performance. Grouped and chronological 
evaluation, richer threat labels, feature ablation, explanation -
fidelity testing, and measured on -device profiling remain the 
necessary next steps before this framework could support a real 
mobile filtering system.
