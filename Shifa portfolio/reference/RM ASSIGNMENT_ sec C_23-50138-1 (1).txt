Abstract 
The July Uprising of 2024 is examined as a pivotal moment 
in Bangladesh’s democratic trajectory that ultimately 
resulted in the collapse of the 16 -year Awami League 
regime. Ongoing debates persist regarding the ownership of 
the movement and the relative contributions of different 
groups. Central to this discussion is the identification of 
key actors and the factors that shaped their roles during the 
uprising. This study analyzes the actors, driving f actors, 
and interrelationships involved in the July Uprising of 2024 
in Bangladesh using a mixed -method approach, including 
literature reviews and convenience surveys (n = 87). 
Guided by stakeholder theory, eleven key actors are 
identified: the Awami Leagu e government; the Anti -
Discrimination Students Movement; Bangladesh Student 
League; YouTubers/bloggers; the general public; media; 
international community/NGOs; opposition political parties 
(BNP, Jamaat-e-Islami, etc.); law and security enforcement 
agencies (RAB, Police, BGB, Bangladesh Army, etc.); the 
Supreme Court; and university authorities. Findings 
indicate that the Anti -Discrimination Students Movement 
and the general public exhibited high power and interest, 
whereas the Awami League government and u niversity 
authorities retained significant power but demonstrated low 
interest. Correlation analysis reveals a positive relationship 
between the interests of the general public and student 
protestors (r = 0.44), a moderate correlation between media 
influence and government power (r = 0.25), and a negative 
correlation between the power of law enforcement agencies 
and student protestors (r = −0.25). The study underscores 
the necessity of inclusive governance and sustained 
political dialogue to address structu ral grievances and 
strengthen democratic values. 
Introduction 
The July uprising, also known as the Bangladesh 2.0 
movement, is a notable instance of  anti-discrimination and 
pro-democracy protests that were primarily started by the 
students of Dhaka University (Chughtai & Ali, 2024). The 
movement‘s initial goal was to change the  quota-based 
system from the government recruitment, especially in light 
of the Supreme  Court‘s verdict to reinstate a 30% quota for 
descendants of freedom fighters. T his ruling  overruled an 
earlier government decision of cancelling all quota systems 
from the  government job that was the result of the 2018 
quota reform movement (Islam et. al., 2024).  Students felt 
that their merit-based opportunities were becoming lim ited, 
and this  reinstatement created a great deal of unhappiness 
among them. Besides, 16 years of division  based on 
political ideology fueled the sentiment of the people which 
resulted into a  widespread protests by the students all over 
the Bangladesh. After the violent clashes and  attacks, the 
situation spiraled out of control, leading to the July 
massacre, which took many  lives. In addition to using 
firearms and sharp weapons, activities in that time included 
the  shutdown of educational institutions , the mobilization 
of the party activists and the law  enforcement agencies 
against each -other. The government effectively cut off 
Bangladesh  from international contacts by enacting a 
nationwide shoot-at-sight curfew and a complete  blackout 
of cell and i nternet connectivity in reaction to the growing 
discontent (Roy, 2024).  As of August 2, there had been 
over thousands of injuries and arrests, and 266 verified  
deaths. UNICEF has recorded the deaths of at least 32 
children during the riots (Mishra, 2024) . The government‘s 
restrictions on hospitals‘ ability to communicate with the 
media, the seizure of hospital security footage, and the 
secret burial of numerous victims of gunshot wounds made 
it more difficult to determine the precise death toll. Awami 
League government claimed that other political groups had 
appropriated the protests for their own purposes. Curfews 
were enforced, but the movement continued and expanded 
its demands, calling for responsibility for state violence, 
banning the student politics , and the resignation of 
important government figures. In the end, the widespread 
violence against civilian s turned the student -led 
demonstrations into a larger movement of non -cooperation, 
which reflected a larger social revolt against the 
government and as a result, ex -Prime Minister Sheikh 
Hasina resigned and flew to India (Bangladesh: Prime 
Minister Hasina resign, 2024).To date, much scholarly 
attention has been devoted to analyzing the impacts and 
historical context of the July uprising; however, our 
research will focus specifically on the actors and relevant 
factors involved in this movement. We aim to explore the 
various actors, including government entities, student 
organizations, anti-discrimination movement platform, and 
international organizations or Non -Government 
Organizations (NGO), etc. while examining their 
interrelations and the dynamics of power and interests at 
play. Initially, we will elucidate the significance of the 
movement within the broader socio -political landscape, 
followed by a com prehensive examination of its 
background. This analysis will extend to understanding 
how the initial nine -point agenda of the movement 
coalesced into a singular focus, propelled by the concerted 
efforts of diverse actors. Subsequently, we will delve into 
the specific power structures and interests of each actor by 
assessing how these factors influenced their actions and 
decisions throughout the uprising. Finally, we will provide 
strategic recommendations for political parties on 
navigating complex social mo vements and addressing the 
concerns of different actors effectively with the 
understanding of the correlation coefficient (r) matrix of 
different actors. 
Methodology 
A mixed -methods approach was adopted to examine the 
actors involved in the July Uprising o f 2024 in Bangladesh, 
integrating qualitative and quantitative techniques 
(Bryman, 2012; Creswell & Plano Clark, 2011). An 
extensive review of secondary literature was first 
conducted to contextualize the uprising and identify key 
actors. Subsequently, con venience surveys using a closed -
ended questionnaire were administered to assess public 
perceptions of the power and interest of 11 identified 
actors, including the Awami League government, Anti -
Discrimination Students Movement, Bangladesh Student 
League, m edia, law enforcement agencies (RAB, Police, 
BGB, etc.), YouTubers/bloggers, the general public, 
international community/NGOs, opposition political parties 
(BNP, Jamaat -e-Islami, etc.), the Supreme Court, and 
university authorities. 
Survey responses from 8 7 participants (n = 87) were 
analyzed using Excel and SPSS to calculate mean power 
and interest scores for each actor. The sample included 45 
males and 42 females, comprising students, protesters, 
teachers, and political activists. A stakeholder matrix was  
constructed to classify actors into four categories based on 
power and interest levels. In addition, a correlation matrix 
analysis was conducted to examine the direction and 
strength of relationships among actors. This 
methodological approach enabled a st ructured assessment 
of actor dynamics during the uprising. 
Literature Review 
The July Uprising of 2024 in Bangladesh has been widely 
viewed as a collective response to prolonged political 
repression, corruption, and human rights violations under 
the Awami League government. The immediate catalyst 
was the Supreme Court’s reinstatement of a quota system in 
government employment favoring descendants of liberation 
fighters, which was broadly perceived as discriminatory. 
Rising youth unemployment, economic pressure, and 
restricted access to merit -based opportunities intensified 
dissatisfaction, particularly among students (Gupta, 2024). 
Reports by the International Labour Organization indicated 
increasing youth participation in the labor force alongside 
limited employment opportunities, further exacerbating 
grievances. Violent crackdowns by security forces escalated 
tensions, transforming demands for quota reform into a 
broader call for democracy, justice, and political 
accountability, including the resignation of Prime Minister 
Sheikh Hasina (Prakash, 2024).The uprising has been 
analyzed through established social movement theories. 
Relative Deprivation Theory (Gurr, 2011) explains the 
mobilization as a response to the gap between expectations 
of merit-based advancement and the reality of quota -based 
exclusion. Resource Mobilization Theory  (McCarthy & 
Zald, 1977) highlights the role of social media, university 
networks, and organizational resources in enabling rapid 
coordination and nationwide protest.State repression 
theories further explain the escalation of the movement. 
According to Til ly (1978), increased state violence often 
intensifies resistance, a pattern reflected in the 
transformation of student protests into a mass uprising 
following police and Chhatra League violence. Davenport’s 
theory of coercive state violence (2007) similarl y suggests 
that tactics such as paramilitary deployment, internet 
blackouts, and shoot -at-sight curfews may suppress dissent 
temporarily but often provoke broader opposition.Political 
Opportunity Theory (Tarrow, 1998) identifies the Supreme 
Court ruling as  a critical opening for mobilization, while 
Framing Theory (Snow & Benford, 1988) explains how the 
quota issue was reframed as resistance to authoritarianism, 
expanding support beyond students. International norm 
dynamics theory (Finnemore & Sikkink, 1998)  further 
contextualizes the movement, as condemnation by 
international organizations increased external pressure on 
the government. 
Finally, theories of hybrid regimes and democratic 
backsliding (Levitsky & Way, 2010) and Economic Protest 
Theory (Kriesi, 1 995) situate the uprising within broader 
patterns of authoritarian governance, economic grievance, 
and mass mobilization. To systematically analyze actor 
influence within this context, stakeholder matrix analysis is 
applied to assess power –interest relatio nships among key 
participants (Frooman, 1999). 
Data Presentation 
Context: Sixteen Years of Awami League Rule 
The July Uprising of 2024 is widely regarded as the 
outcome of prolonged political repression, economic 
hardship, and democratic erosion during the  16-year rule of 
the Awami League under Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina. 
The period was marked by deep political polarization, 
pervasive corruption, and a narrowing of democratic space, 
which collectively generated widespread public discontent 
(Rishi, 2024). Political control was reinforced through the 
promotion of a “Freedom Fighters versus Others” narrative, 
which framed opposition groups as anti -national and 
intensified exclusion and mistrust within the political 
system (Haque, 2024). Consequently, oppositi on parties—
particularly the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) and 
Jamaat-e-Islami—were systematically marginalized. 
Democratic institutions were further weakened through 
disputed national elections in 2014, 2018, and 2024, which 
were characterized by alle gations of voter intimidation, 
manipulation, and suppression, alongside repeated 
opposition boycotts (Siddiqui & Paul, 2019). These 
electoral practices contributed to widespread perceptions of 
disenfranchisement and the collapse of electoral credibility. 
At the same time, civic and political spaces were 
increasingly constrained. Independent media, civil society 
organizations, and dissenting voices were subjected to 
censorship, harassment, and legal persecution, allowing 
state authority to be consolidated wi th limited 
accountability (The State of Media Freedom in Bangladesh, 
2021). Although periods of economic growth were 
recorded, structural economic problems —including 
inequality, corruption, rising living costs, and post -
pandemic pressures —persisted. High -profile governance 
failures and corruption scandals further eroded public trust 
(Taylor, 2016; Mustafa, 2024). 
Youth dissatisfaction intensified amid limited employment 
opportunities and restricted political participation. Despite 
earlier political disengag ement, students emerged as 
leading actors in 2024, mobilized primarily by opposition 
to discriminatory job quotas favoring descendants of 
liberation fighters. Ultimately, prolonged repression, 
economic strain, and institutional decay converged to 
produce t he July Uprising, representing a critical turning 
point in Bangladesh’s political history, with its long -term 
democratic outcomes remaining uncertain. 
 
Discrimination in Politics and Society During the 
Awami League Regime 
From 2008 to 2024, Bangladesh’s political and social 
environment was shaped by systemic favoritism and 
exclusion under the Awami League government. Public -
sector recruitment was widely perceived as politicized, 
particularly through the civil service quota system that 
reserved 30 percent of positions for descendants of 
liberation fighters. Although justified as historical 
recognition, the policy was criticized for privileging 
political affiliation over merit and undermining institutional 
credibility (Corea &  Erum, 2024). The marginalization of 
qualified candidates contributed to widespread 
dissatisfaction and fueled the quota reform movement led 
primarily by students and young people.Discriminatory 
practices were also reported in post -examination 
recruitment processes, where successful Bangladesh Civil 
Service candidates were denied appointments due to 
politically influenced police verification procedures. In 
parallel, dissent was suppressed through legal mechanisms 
such as the Digital Security Act and the ICT  Act, which 
were used to detain journalists, activists, and critics of the 
government, fostering a climate of fear and restricting 
freedom of expression (Almond, 2019).Public trust was 
further eroded by pervasive corruption, particularly in the 
banking sector, where large -scale embezzlement involving 
individuals linked to the ruling party was reported. 
Allegations of corruption within the Election Commission 
also weakened confidence in democratic governance. 
Collectively, political discrimination, repressio n of dissent, 
and institutional corruption generated widespread 
frustration and contributed to the mass protests of July –
August 2024, marking a decisive rupture in Bangladesh’s 
political landscape. 
 
July Uprising 2024 and Its Aftermath 
The July Uprising of 2024 in Bangladesh was the result of 
prolonged political repression, economic exclusion, and 
institutional corruption under the Awami League 
government, which had ruled for more than 16 years. 
Democratic norms were progressively weaken ed through 
restrictions on press freedom, limitations on free 
expression, and the use of law enforcement agencies to 
suppress dissent. These practices generated widespread 
resentment, particularly among young people who 
experienced political marginalization and limited economic 
opportunity.Despite sustained economic growth, 
employment opportunities remained constrained, especially 
for educated youth. Although Bangladesh was frequently 
cited as a high -growth economy, the benefits were 
unevenly distributed, l eaving an estimated 2.6 million 
young people unemployed (Zaman, 2024). This disconnect 
between economic performance and lived realities 
intensified public dissatisfaction. Public trust was further 
eroded by persistent allegations of corruption, reflected i n 
Bangladesh’s low ranking in Transparency International’s 
2023 Corruption Perception Index.The reinstatement of the 
government job quota system served as the immediate 
catalyst for mass mobilization. Under this system, 56 
percent of public -sector positions were reserved, including 
30 percent for descendants of liberation fighters (Ganguly, 
2024). Although originally intended to address historical 
inequalities, the policy was increasingly perceived as 
discriminatory and incompatible with merit -based 
recruitment. The High Court’s decision in June 2024 to 
reinstate the quota system triggered widespread student 
protests.Demonstrations began at Dhaka University on July 
1, 2024, and rapidly expanded nationwide. Initial demands 
centered on quota reform; however, t he movement 
escalated following violent state responses, including 
inflammatory rhetoric, the mobilization of party -affiliated 
groups, and the use of force by security agencies. Student 
deaths, notably that of Abu Sayeed in Rangpur, intensified 
public outrage and transformed the protests into a broader 
anti-authoritarian movement.As curfews were imposed and 
communication networks disrupted, the Anti -
Discrimination Students Movement expanded its demands 
to include accountability for state violence, the bann ing of 
the Bangladesh Chhatra League, and the resignation of 
senior officials. The uprising ultimately evolved into a 
nationwide non-cooperation movement, culminating in the 
resignation of Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina. The events of 
July 2024 underscored t he capacity of youth -led 
mobilization to challenge entrenched political power and 
reaffirmed the central importance of democratic 
accountability and social justice. 
 
Reform to Revolution: From Nine -Point to One -Point 
Demand 
The July Uprising of 2024 was led by the Anti -
Discrimination Students Movement and initially focused on 
reforming the government job quota system. The quota 
policy was widely viewed as unfair and harmful to merit -
based recruitment. Students demanded the ca ncellation of 
the existing quota system and the introduction of limited 
and fair quotas for marginalized groups.After the Supreme 
Court reinstated a 30 percent quota for descendants of 
liberation fighters, student protests spread rapidly across 
universities. The movement escalated sharply when 
protests were met with violence by law enforcement 
agencies and the ruling party’s student wing, the 
Bangladesh Chhatra League. Student deaths and injuries 
marked a turning point and led to an expansion of 
demands.A nine-point demand was later announced, calling 
for a public apology from the Prime Minister, the 
resignation of responsible ministers, punishment of police 
officials involved in violence, the resignation of university 
authorities, and a ban on the Bangladesh Chhatra League in 
educational institutions. These demands shifted the 
movement from quota reform to accountability for state 
violence.As repression continued, the movement adopted a 
non-cooperation strategy and consolidated its demands into 
a single poin t: the resignation of Prime Minister Sheikh 
Hasina and her cabinet. This shift marked the 
transformation of the movement from reform -oriented 
protest to a broader revolutionary struggle for democratic 
accountability. 
 
Opposition Parties (BNP, Jamaat-e-Islami, etc.) 
The Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) and Jamaat -e-
Islami were indirectly involved in the July Uprising 
following prolonged political repression under the Awami 
League government. Over the previous 16 years, party 
leaders and activists —particularly from Jamaat -e-Islami—
were subjected to arrests, trials, executions, and systematic 
suppression, which fostered long -term resentment and 
organizational resilience.During the uprising, experienced 
activists from both parties contributed to sustaining 
mobilization, largely at the grassroots level. Individuals 
associated with Islami Chhatra Shibir were involved in 
coordination and organizational support behind the scenes. 
BNP maintained a strategic distance by allowing student 
groups to lead publicly, there by reducing the risk of 
intensified state repression and preserving the movement’s 
legitimacy.Although the uprising appeared student -led, the 
background support, experience, and strategic restraint of 
BNP and Jamaat -e-Islami played a significant role in 
maintaining momentum and continuity.

The power–interest analysis (Figure 2) illustrates variations 
in influence and engagement among key actors during the 
July Uprising. The Awami League government displayed 
relatively high power (3.10) but low interest (2.41), 
indicating limited responsiveness to public and student 
demands. Violent repression and dismissive rhetoric 
reflected this disengagement and contributed to protest 
escalation.In contrast, the Anti -Discrimination Students 
Movement demonstrated the highest levels of power (4.31) 
and interest (4.62), reflecting strong mobilization capacity, 
nationwide coordination, and sustained commitment. The 
general public also showed high power (3.90) and interest 
(4.28), parti cularly as state violence intensified and the 
movement evolved into a broader struggle for democratic 
rights.Media actors recorded moderately high power (3.52) 
and interest (3.79), though their influence was constrained 
by censorship and intimidation. YouT ubers and bloggers 
exhibited moderate power (2.86) and interest (3.38), 
primarily through online narrative framing. The 
international community and NGOs demonstrated  
relatively high power (3.79) and interest (3.90), as external 
pressure and human rights advocacy intensified. Opposition 
political parties showed moderate power (2.93) and interest 
(3.55), reflecting indirect and strategic involvement. Law 
enforcement agencies exhibited moderate power (2.88) and 
low interest (3.00), with their role foc used on enforcement 
rather than engagement. A negative correlation between the 
power of law enforcement agencies and student protestors 
(r = −0.25) highlights the adversarial relationship between 
the state and the movement. 
 
Key Actors and Their Roles in the July Uprising 
During the July Uprising of 2024, multiple actors with 
varying levels of power and interest shaped the course of 
events. The Awami League government, despite possessing 
high institutional power, demonstrated low interest in 
addressing prot est demands and relied primarily on 
repression, deploying police, RAB, and BGB to suppress 
demonstrations, which resulted in extensive casualties and 
public outrage. The Bangladesh Student League, aligned 
with the ruling party, opposed the movement through  
violent actions against student protestors, triggering 
nationwide student resistance. Law enforcement agencies 
acted largely as instruments of state control, employing 
force against protestors, while their actions intensified 
public anger and contributed to the escalation of the 
uprising. The Supreme Court played a pivotal role by 
reinstating the controversial quota system, which was 
widely perceived as undermining meritocracy and served as 
a direct catalyst for mass mobilization. In contrast, the 
Anti-Discrimination Students Movement emerged as the 
central driving force, exhibiting high power and high 
interest through coordinated nationwide protests led by 
student and youth organizers. University authorities largely 
remained disengaged or acted to contain protests, whereas 
sections of university teachers actively supported student 
safety and demands. The general public became a powerful 
stakeholder as outrage over state violence led to widespread 
participation beyond student circles. Media outlets played a 
divided role, with independent channels amplifying protest 
realities while state -aligned media minimized dissent. 
YouTubers and bloggers, despite limited formal power, 
significantly influenced public perception through digital 
platforms. Finally, internati onal organizations and NGOs 
applied external pressure by condemning human rights 
violations, reinforcing the legitimacy of the movement and 
increasing global scrutiny of state actions.

Clustering the Actors and Their Actions in July Uprising 
The July Uprising of 2024 in Bangladesh was a 
significant moment in  the country‘s political landscape, 
marked by various actors whose ideologies and actions 
either supported or opposed the movement. The 
dynamics among these actors highlight the complexities 
and tensions inherent in the struggle for de mocratic 
rights and social justice. On one side, the Awami League 
Government, its student wing (the Student League), 
certain media outlets, law enforcement agencies, the 
Supreme Court, and university authorities were aligned 
against the protests, advocatin g for suppression. 
Conversely, the Anti-discrimination Students Movement, 
select Youtubers and bloggers, segments of the general 
public, supportive media, international organizations, 
opposition political parties, and the Bangladesh Army 
emerged as allies of the movement. 
 
University teachers’ networks 
The Awami League government was centrally positioned 
among actors opposing the uprising. The state response was 
characterized by dismissive rhetoric and coercive 
suppression, with law enforcement agencies dep loyed 
against student protests. Violent confrontations, particularly 
on July 16, resulted in injuries and fatalities and intensified 
public outrage. The Bangladesh Student League, as the 
ruling party’s student wing, was actively involved in 
countering student protestors, often through confrontational 
means. Law enforcement agencies, including the police and 
RAB, were primarily engaged in enforcing government 
directives, frequently through force rather than dialogue. 
Reports of violence against protestors an d journalists 
further reflected an increasingly repressive 
environment.Institutional actors such as the Supreme Court 
and university authorities were also perceived as aligned 
against the movement. The Supreme Court’s decision to 
reinstate the quota system acted as a key trigger for protests 
and was widely interpreted as reinforcing state authority 
over merit -based concerns. University authorities were 
criticized for failing to protect students and for their limited 
engagement during episodes of violence on  campuses.In 
contrast, the Anti -Discrimination Students Movement 
emerged as the core pro -uprising actor, leading 
mobilization and sustaining protests nationwide. The 
movement expanded rapidly following violent crackdowns 
and student deaths, transforming in itial quota -related 
demands into broader calls for accountability. Support from 
segments of the general public increased as repression 
intensified, reflecting shared grievances regarding 
governance and democratic rights. Select YouTubers and 
bloggers playe d a supportive role by disseminating 
information and shaping public narratives, while supportive 
media outlets amplified protest -related 
coverage.International organizations and NGOs contributed 
by condemning state violence and highlighting human 
rights concerns, thereby increasing external pressure on the 
government. Opposition political parties remained largely 
in the background but were positioned as supportive 
stakeholders. Notably, the Bangladesh Army, initially 
neutral, was increasingly perceived as s ympathetic to 
public sentiment in the later stages of the uprising.Overall, 
the clustering reveals a clear division between state -aligned 
institutions seeking to suppress dissent and a broad 
coalition of students, citizens, and supportive actors 
advocating for reform, accountability, and democratic 
rights. 
 
Discussion 
This study was conducted to analyze the factors, key actors, 
and interrelationships that shaped the July Uprising of 2024 
in Bangladesh, with particular focus on the correlation 
between actors’ power and interest. Eleven key 
stakeholders were identified, i ncluding state institutions, 
student groups, media, political actors, civil society, and 
international organizations.High levels of power and 
interest were observed among the Anti -Discrimination 
Students Movement and the general public, enabling 
sustained mobilization and broad societal support. In 
contrast, the Awami League government and university 
authorities retained considerable institutional power but 
demonstrated limited interest in addressing protest 
demands, indicating a clear institutional –public disconnect. 
The Bangladesh Student League was aligned with the 
government, while the media landscape remained divided 
between state -aligned and independent reporting. External 
pressure was applied by international organizations and 
NGOs, while opposition p olitical parties remained 
supportive but largely indirect. Law enforcement agencies 
were primarily engaged in enforcing state directives, and 
judicial actions by the Supreme Court became key points of 
contention.Correlation analysis indicated a positive 
relationship between public and student protestors’ interests 
(r = 0.44), reflecting strong societal alignment. A moderate 
correlation between media power and government authority 
(r = 0.25) suggested state influence over information flows, 
while a negative correlation between law enforcement 
power and student protestors (r = −0.25) highlighted the 
adversarial impact of state repression on grassroots 
mobilization.Overall, the findings were found to be 
consistent with social movement and state repression 
theories, while also demonstrating how local political 
dynamics shaped the trajectory of the July Uprising in 
Bangladesh. 
 
Conclusion 
The July Uprising of 2024 is widely regarded as a defining 
moment in Bangladesh’s pursuit of democracy, 
accountability, and soc ial justice. Through an actor –factor 
matrix analysis, eleven key actors were identified, 
revealing varied levels of power and interest and 
highlighting complex interactions among state institutions, 
student movements, the public, media, and international 
actors. Although the uprising was initially triggered by 
quota-related grievances, it evolved into a broader 
resistance against authoritarian practices and democratic 
erosion as public support intensified.The findings 
emphasize the necessity of constructive  political 
engagement during crises. Repression was shown to 
exacerbate conflict, whereas dialogue, transparency, and 
responsiveness were identified as essential for addressing 
grievances and restoring public trust. The importance of 
inclusive governance, institutional accountability, and 
independent oversight —particularly regarding law 
enforcement conduct—was underscored.Overall, while the 
July Uprising reflected deep -rooted societal discontent, it 
also presented an opportunity for democratic renewal. By 
fostering inclusive participation, protecting civic space, and 
respecting diverse viewpoints, a more accountable and 
responsive governance framework may be advanced in 
Bangladesh.
